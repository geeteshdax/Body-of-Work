# BUSINESS UNDERSTANDING

**Read Time:** 8 minutes  
**Key Takeaway:** HDFC makes money on one number: the spread between deposit costs and lending rates. When that spread narrows, the entire business slows.  
**Who Should Read This:** Anyone deciding whether HDFC is a good business or just another bank

---

## The Insight

HDFC Bank's business is simple. Take money from savers at one price (5–6%), lend it to borrowers at a higher price (9–10%), and keep the difference (3.4%). Repeat this cycle 100 million times across your customer base. That difference—called the interest margin or NIM—is everything. 

When the spread widens, HDFC thrives. When it narrows, HDFC struggles. Right now, it's narrowing. That's the entire story.

Most bank analysis gets complicated with ratios and acronyms. Strip away the noise: HDFC is a spread business. The spread is under pressure. Question becomes: Is it temporary pressure or structural?

---

## Why This Matters

The spread is the moat. It's what makes HDFC defensible.

Here's why: If HDFC can consistently borrow money cheaper than competitors, it can either:
- Lend at better rates (attract more customers), OR
- Keep wider spreads (earn more profit per rupee lent)

A 3.4% spread today means HDFC earns roughly ₹3.4 crores profit for every ₹100 crores it lends. Do that across ₹26.5 trillion in loans, and you're generating massive earnings.

But spreads aren't magic. They exist only when:
1. Savers trust you more than competitors (pay you lower rates)
2. Borrowers believe you're the best lender (accept higher rates)
3. You have scale (make volume work at thin margins)

HDFC had all three. Now it's losing two.

---

## Evidence & Reasoning

**What's happening to the spread:**

| What HDFC Pays on Deposits | What It Earns on Loans | The Spread (NIM) |
|---|---|---|
| FY25: 4.8% | 8.3% | 3.54% |
| FY26: 5.2% | 8.6% | 3.38% |
| Change | +40 bps up | +30 bps up | –16 bps down |

The spread compressed. Not by much in absolute terms, but by a lot in percentage terms (–4.5% of the spread itself).

**Why deposits got expensive (structural incentive):**

Competitors (ICICI, Axis, Kotak) are fighting for the same deposits. ICICI now offers 6.5% on fixed deposits where HDFC offers 5.8%. Savers switch for 0.7% more. HDFC has to match or lose the money. When rivals all raise rates, nobody wins—but depositors do. HDFC's cost of funding rises.

This isn't temporary price competition. This is structural. Every time RBI keeps interest rates stable (not cutting), deposit competition stays fierce. HDFC's cost-of-deposits advantage—which was enormous five years ago—is mostly gone.

**Why loan yields fell (competitive + structural):**

Mortgages are the most competitive segment. HDFC offers 8.5–9%, but housing finance NBFCs (non-bank lenders) offer 8.2–8.8%. Why would a borrower choose HDFC for 0.5% less yield? They don't, unless HDFC lowers rates. HDFC lowers rates. Volume stays same, but spread shrinks.

Corporate loans? Even worse. Large companies refinance constantly. HDFC lending to corporates at 7.5% this quarter might see that same corporate refinance elsewhere at 6.8% next quarter. HDFC loses the customer or matches the rate.

Result: Loan yields slowly decline as competition intensifies.

**The incentive problem:**

Everyone in banking faces this. It's not unique to HDFC. When the industry has excess deposit-taking capacity and loan demand grows slower than deposits, rates fall. This is a **cycle**, not a one-time event. It will continue until either:
- Loan demand surges (economic boom), OR
- Some banks fail (industry consolidation reduces competitors), OR
- Interest rates rise (RBI tightens policy)

None of these seem imminent.

---

## The Implication

A spread compressed from 3.54% to 3.38% means profit growth is slowing.

Do the math: If HDFC lends ₹26.5 trillion at 8.6% and funds it at 5.2%, it earns about ₹900 billion in interest income. Now compress that spread to 3.38%. It earns ₹900 billion less in new spreads. To grow interest income at the same rate as before, HDFC needs **higher loan growth** to offset the lower spread per loan.

But loan growth is slowing (12% vs. historical 15–18%). So profit growth is slowing too. HDFC's net profit grew only 4.9% last year. That's the real story: not that the bank is broke, but that the **growth engine is sputtering**.

For an investor, slower growth + fair valuation = low return. This is why the stock is fairly valued at ₹775, not cheap.

---

## What to Watch Quarterly

| Metric | Current | Target (Stabilization) | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|---|
| **NIM** | 3.38% | 3.55–3.60% | Falls below 3.30% |
| **Deposit Cost** | 5.2% | Stays 5–5.2% | Rises above 5.5% |
| **Loan Yield** | 8.6% | Stays 8.5–8.6% | Falls below 8.4% |
| **Loan Growth** | 12% | Stays 11–13% | Falls below 10% |
| **CASA Ratio** | 34.1% | Stabilizes 35%+ | Falls below 33% |

The spread is a lagging indicator. Watch CASA first. If deposit competition forces CASA lower (savers moving to competitors for higher fixed rates), deposit costs will rise further, and spread will compress more.
