# STAKEHOLDER PERSPECTIVES

**Read Time:** 10 minutes  
**Key Takeaway:** HDFC looks different depending on who you are. Here's what each stakeholder should actually care about.  
**Who Should Read This:** Choose your lens  Founder, Competitor, Strategy Team or Investor

---

## FOR FOUNDERS

**What matters to you:** Capital allocation discipline. How does HDFC deploy capital, and what can you learn?

**The insight:** HDFC's capital decisions are **rational, not brilliant.** The merger with HDFC Limited (2023) doubled the balance sheet and created a combined entity. Rational move—consolidates the franchise. But it diluted RoE for 2+ years (equity doubled, profits haven't caught up). That's a trade-off HDFC accepted for long-term scale. 

The dividend policy (40% payout, 60% retention) is textbook prudent capital allocation. HDFC isn't trying to milk the business or hide bad decisions. It's building for the long term.

But here's the lesson: **Even excellent management makes trade-offs that hurt near-term returns.** HDFC accepted lower RoE (15% instead of 17%) for 2+ years to strengthen the balance sheet and capture more customer wallet share. That takes conviction. Most founders would skip the merger because it hurt near-term EPS.

The governance failure (AT-1 mis-selling) is the counter-lesson: **Good capital allocation doesn't prevent bad control systems.** HDFC's board approved a product (AT-1 bonds to retail) that made financial sense (fees) but violated customer trust. Someone prioritized near-term revenue over long-term reputation. That's how even good companies stumble.

**What to study:** How HDFC balances growth (12% loan growth, mergers, spinoffs) with stability (conservative provisioning, strong CAR). How it prioritizes. How it kills projects (hard to see, but happening).

**Strategic question:** When should you accept lower near-term returns to build long-term competitive advantage? And how do you prevent your organization from optimizing metrics (revenue per product) instead of values (customer trust)?

## FOR COMPETITORS (ICICI, Axis, Kotak)

**What matters to you:** Where is HDFC weak? How do we attack?

**The insight:** HDFC is weak in **three specific places**: (1) Deposit cost (5.2% average, vs. your 5.0%), (2) Mortgage pricing (8.5–9%, vs. your 8.2–8.4%), (3) Corporate client loyalty (lowest RoE among peers suggests weaker corporate franchise). HDFC is strong in distribution and retail brand, which are hard to attack.

Your competitive advantage: You're faster on rates. When HDFC raises mortgage rates to 9.2%, you're at 8.8% and winning new customers. When deposit rates rise, you absorb the cost hit faster because you're smaller and more agile. HDFC is a battleship; you're a speedboat.

HDFC's governance event is your opportunity. For 18 months, HDFC customers (especially deposit holders) are slightly nervous about trust. That's a window to win CASA deposits, especially from high-net-worth and corporate customers who care about governance.

**What to monitor:** HDFC's CASA ratio (currently 34.1%). If it stays flat or drops, you're winning the deposit war. Monitor mortgage market share (HDFC losing share to NBFCs). Monitor RoE trend (if it's stuck at 15% while you're at 17%, perception gap widens—customers prefer you). Monitor management departures (if CFO or CRO leaves, instability signal; your chance to recruit).

**Your attack plan:** (1) Aggressive deposit rate offers to high-net-worth segment (currently HDFC's sticky base). (2) Undercut mortgage rates by 30–50 bps for 12 months (steal market share, rebuild later). (3) Hire HDFC's best branch managers and relationship officers (governance distraction makes them vulnerable). (4) Market your governance strength ("We've never had a chair resign").

**Strategic question:** Do we attack HDFC's moat by going after deposits (slow, expensive) or by capturing mortgages from NBFCs (faster, cheaper way to grow)?

## FOR STRATEGY TEAMS

**What matters to you:** What are the strategic inflection points? What should we be monitoring?

**The insight:** Indian banking is entering a **new equilibrium.** For 15 years (2008–2023), the game was "who can grow fastest?" Larger banks won because they had deposit access. HDFC won because it was largest.

Now the game is "who can maintain margins while growing?" This favors banks with:
- Operational leverage (cost discipline) — HDFC 39.5%, improving
- Pricing power (diversified income, not just NIM) — HDFC weak here (only 20% non-interest income)
- Customer stickiness (digital + brand) — HDFC strong but eroding

The strategic shift: **Deposit wars are permanent.** They won't resolve when rates go up—they'll just shift form. HDFC's cost-of-deposits advantage (once its primary moat) is gone for good. The new moat is **operational efficiency** (who can serve customers cheaper?) and **customer lifetime value** (who can cross-sell, not just lend?).

HDFC is good at efficiency (cost-to-income improving). It's weak at cross-selling (mortgage customers not converting to CASA, low fee income growth). That's where it'll be vulnerable for the next 5 years.

**What to monitor quarterly:**
- **NIM trend** — Watch for stabilization. If it stays compressed, structural change confirmed.
- **CASA trend** — Proxy for competitive intensity. Rising = war escalating.
- **Fee income growth** — HDFC's weak spot. If it stays 5–8%, bank is losing ability to monetize customer relationships.
- **Management departures** — Governance wound still open?
- **Regulatory guidance** — Will RBI tighten CAR post-governance event? Will it affect lending capacity?
- **Market share trends** — Mortgages, MSME, corporate. Is HDFC holding or losing?

**Your strategic question:** If deposit wars force all private banks into a lower-margin equilibrium (3.5% NIM instead of historical 3.8%), how does the sector adapt? Do banks consolidate? Do some exit? Or do all accept 13–14% RoE as the new normal and move on?

## FOR INVESTORS

**What matters to you:** Capital return. Can you buy at ₹800 and sell at ₹1,100 in 3–5 years?

**The insight:** Not right now. HDFC at ₹830 is fairly valued, not cheap. You'd need RoE recovery to 16%+ AND a multiple re-rating to 1.83x+ (from current 2.27x). That's a two-step climb. Most likely scenario: stock trades sideways at ₹800–₹850 for 2 years while management executes. By FY27–28, if NIM recovers and RoE improves, fair value rises to ₹700+. Then you have 7–10% annual returns.

That's fine for a core holding. It's not exciting for capital appreciation.

**What to monitor:** The four recovery drivers: NIM (target 3.55%+), CASA (target stabilization at 35%), Cost-to-Income (target 38%), RoE (target 16%). If all four trend right by Q2 FY27, your conviction improves and fair value re-rates. If any breaks, your thesis breaks.

**Your action:** Build a position at ₹600–₹700 (attractive risk/reward), not ₹830. Patience is profitable. Current price is paying for a recovery that hasn't happened yet.

**Strategic question:** Are you buying HDFC for 5% annual dividend + 6% growth (11% total return)? Or are you waiting for a chance at 15%+ returns via a margin of safety entry?
