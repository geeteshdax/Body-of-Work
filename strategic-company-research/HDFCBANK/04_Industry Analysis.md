# INDUSTRY ANALYSIS

**Read Time:** 7 minutes  
**Key Takeaway:** Indian banking is in a margin war. Four strong banks are fighting for the same deposits. HDFC is winning on scale but losing on pricing power.  
**Who Should Read This:** Founders understanding competitive dynamics, strategists monitoring sector trends, investors assessing HDFC's positioning

---

## The Insight

The Indian banking sector isn't growing—it's consolidating. Four private banks (HDFC, ICICI, Axis, Kotak) control most of the market. They're all profitable. They're all growing. But they're also all fighting each other for the same deposits and the same loan customers. When strong competitors fight over finite resources, margins compress. That's happening now.

HDFC is the biggest player by assets (₹682 Tr). But size isn't winning this fight. Speed and aggression are. ICICI is more aggressive on deposit rates. Axis is more aggressive on lending. Kotak maintains premium positioning but at smaller scale. HDFC is stuck in the middle: largest but not fastest, strongest but not most aggressive.

---

## Why This Matters

Banking sectors go through cycles. This one is in a **margin compression cycle**. It happens when:

1. Loan demand grows slower than deposit supply (money chasing fewer borrowers)
2. Competitors have excess capital (all four banks do)
3. No consolidation happens (all four survive, all four fight)

When this happens, banks race to the bottom on rates. Deposit rates rise (savers win), loan rates fall (borrowers win), and bank margins get squeezed (banks lose).

HDFC thrived in the **margin expansion cycle** (2014–2020) when it had size advantages and competitors were weaker. Now it's in a compression cycle where size isn't enough.

---

## Evidence & Reasoning

**Sector snapshot (FY26):**

| Bank | Loan Growth | Deposit Growth | NIM | CASA Ratio | RoE |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **HDFC** | 12% | 14.4% | 3.38% | 34.1% | 15.1% |
| **ICICI** | 15.8% | 11.4% | 4.32% | 38.6% | 17–18% |
| **Axis** | 18.5% | 14% | 3.62% | 40% | ~16% |
| **Kotak** | 16% | 14.7% | 4.67% | 43.3% | 17–18% |

**Reading this table tells the story:**

HDFC is growing deposits faster than loans (14.4% vs. 12%). This surplus deposit money has to go somewhere. HDFC lends less of it, keeps more as reserve. Why? Because loan market is competitive—rates are falling. Better to sit on deposits than lend at low rates.

Meanwhile, ICICI and Axis are growing loans faster (15–18%). They're willing to lend at tighter margins because they have different strategies. ICICI has corporate/investment banking (higher margins). Axis is gaining market share post-Citibank acquisition (growth matters more than margins).

**Why HDFC's NIM is the lowest:**

HDFC's CASA ratio (34.1%) is the lowest. CASA deposits are cheap (current/savings accounts, 3–4% cost). Time deposits are expensive (5–6.5% cost). Lower CASA ratio = higher average deposit cost. Higher deposit cost = squeezed spread.

Why is HDFC's CASA ratio so low? Post-merger with HDFC Limited (July 2023), the combined entity inherited mortgage customers (lower CASA affinity) plus lost some corporate CASA to rivals. It's partly accounting (dilution from merger) and partly competitive loss.

**The incentive structure:**

All four banks face the same incentive: grow deposits or raise capital via bonds. HDFC chose deposits. Cost of deposits is rising because rivals are competing. Result: HDFC pays more for deposits than it did 2 years ago. This is structural, not temporary.

Loan demand is steady but not booming (GDP growth ~6.5%, loan growth 12–18% is still ahead of GDP). So banks have more deposits than loans to make. They compete on rates. Whoever needs growth most competes hardest. HDFC needs growth (rebuild RoE post-merger). ICICI needs growth (corporate franchise expanding). Axis needs growth (post-Citibank integration). Kotak is selective (premium positioning). All three aggressive players are pressuring HDFC.

---

## The Implication

HDFC is in a **commoditizing sector** within a margin compression cycle.

Five years ago, HDFC's size (largest bank, most branches, best brand) gave it pricing power. Customers preferred HDFC. Savers accepted lower rates. Borrowers paid higher rates.

Today, that advantage is narrowing. Savers shop for rates (digital banking makes switching easy). Borrowers refinance constantly (corporate clients especially). HDFC's **brand moat is durable but not deep anymore**.

For HDFC shareholders, this means: Profit growth will be slower than deposit/loan growth. The spread (NIM) is the limiting factor, not volume.
