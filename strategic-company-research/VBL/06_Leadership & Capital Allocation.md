# TOPIC 6: LEADERSHIP & CAPITAL ALLOCATION

**Read Time:** 6 minutes  
**Key Takeaway:** Management executes operationally, but the real test is whether Africa capex generates returns above cost of capital.  
**Who Should Read This:** Founders studying capital allocation in growth cycles

---

## The Insight

VBL's management is operationally competent but untested on capital allocation at scale.

**Operational execution:** Proven. Revenue scaled 2.7x in 4 years. Margins stable at 16–20% despite Campa disruption. Won PepsiCo Bottler of Year 2023. CEO change (Nov 2022: Kapul Agarwal → Raj Gandhi) was smooth. No visible execution failures.

**Capital allocation execution:** Unproven. Heavy capex cycle underway (₹200–300 Cr annually on Africa plants, new manufacturing capacity). Africa integration (South Africa, Zambia, Zimbabwe acquired 2023–24) is early-stage.

This distinction matters. VBL can execute operations. Can it execute capital deployment at the scale it's attempting? We don't know yet.

---

## Why This Matters

Capital allocation determines long-term shareholder returns.

VBL is in a **heavy capex cycle.** It's sacrificing near-term FCF (free cash flow is declining despite profit growth) to build manufacturing capacity in India and African infrastructure. This is intentional. The bet: Africa and new plants will generate 20%+ over 3–5 years, justifying today's capex.

This is not a governance scandal risk. This is an **execution risk on capital deployment.**

---

## Evidence & Reasoning

**Management Track Record (Positive)**

| Decision | Year | Outcome | Quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Revenue growth** | FY21–FY25 | 2.7x in 4 years | Excellent execution |
| **Margin stability** | FY22–FY25 | 16–20% despite Campa | Good cost control |
| **Product innovation** | FY24–FY25 | Sting scaling, premiumization working | Strategic thinking |
| **Operational efficiency** | Ongoing | Bottler of Year 2023 | PepsiCo validation |
| **CEO succession** | Nov 2022 | Smooth transition (Agarwal → Gandhi) | Governance competence |

**Management's Capex Decisions (Untested)**

| Investment | Amount | Timeline | Expected ROCE | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Africa Plants** | ₹1,500–2,000 Cr | FY23–FY26 | 18–22% (target) | In progress; unproven |
| **India Capacity** | ₹1,000+ Cr | FY25–FY27 | 20%+ (target) | Expected to complete |
| **New Product Lines** | ₹300–400 Cr | Ongoing | 22–25% (Sting, health) | Ramping, promising |
| **Working Capital** | Growing | Ongoing | Implicit in operations | Normal |

**Translation:** Management is betting ₹3,000+ Cr (capex) that Africa will deliver 18–22% ROCE and new product lines will hit 22–25% ROCE. Historical ROCE on India operations (23–26%) suggests management has execution capability. But Africa is new territory.

**Debt Rising (The Financing Mechanism)**

| Metric | FY24 | FY25 | Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Debt/Equity** | 0.17x | 0.79x | Rising sharply (deliberate capex financing) |
| **Net Debt** | Low | Rising | Financing expansion via borrowing |
| **Interest Coverage** | Strong | Adequate | Manageable but monitored |
| **FCF** | Declining | Declining | Most cash reinvested, not paid out |

**Why debt is rising:** VBL is borrowing to fund Africa and India capex. This is rational if capex ROCE exceeds cost of debt (7–8%). But it's a leverage on unproven returns.

---

## The Implication

VBL is in a **classic capex cycle:** Heavy investment in FY25–FY26, then capex moderating in FY27–FY28, then FCF recovering as investments mature.

**This is normal for growth businesses.** The question is whether management is disciplined about capital allocation or just spending money hoping it works.

**Evidence of discipline:**
- Africa plants are real (not speculative). Built capacity to serve markets.
- Sting investment is strategic (high-margin, growing segment).
- Debt is measured (0.79x is elevated but not excessive for ROCE 20%+).

**Risk signals:**
- Africa ROCE unproven (could turn out 12–15% instead of 18–22%).
- Capex cycle extending longer than guided (FY26 instead of FY25).
- FCF staying negative (not recovering as expected).
- Debt rising faster than planned.

Any of these would signal capital allocation failure.
